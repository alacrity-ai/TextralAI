# What survived the Library of Alexandria

The Library of Alexandria, founded in the third century BCE under
Ptolemy I Soter and his son Ptolemy II Philadelphus, was for
several centuries the largest single collection of texts in the
ancient Mediterranean. Its catalog at peak is variously estimated
at between 100,000 and 700,000 papyrus rolls — figures that should
be read as orders of magnitude rather than precise counts.

The library's destruction is more myth than event. There was no
single catastrophic fire that ended the institution overnight. The
collection eroded across several centuries: Caesar's accidental
harbor fire in 48 BCE consumed an unknown but substantial portion
of the holdings; the city's cycles of riot and reconquest under
Aurelian and Diocletian damaged successive parts of it; and the
slow drift of scholarship toward Constantinople and Antioch left
the remaining holdings increasingly neglected.

What survived was carried in copies. The texts most thoroughly
preserved are those that scholars had already copied widely:
Euclid's Elements, the Homeric epics, the major works of Aristotle,
and the medical compendia of Galen all survived because they had
been transmitted through hundreds of independent chains. The
Library's role as a single irreplaceable repository was always
weaker than its reputation suggests; its real function was as a
gathering place for the scholars who did the copying.

The most poignant losses are catalogued only by reference. Many
plays of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides are known only from
the lists of titles preserved in later commentaries. The
mathematical works of Apollonius beyond the Conics survive only as
fragments. The history of the world before the Greeks, as Berossus
and Manetho recorded it, comes down to us through quotations in
later authors.

The library's true legacy is the model of scholarship it
established: state-sponsored research, systematic cataloging, and
the accumulation of texts as a deliberate project. That model
moved to Constantinople, then to the monasteries of medieval
Europe, and eventually to the universities of the modern world.
