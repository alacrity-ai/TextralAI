# The decline of the Library

The decline of the Library of Alexandria was not a single event
but a cycle of damage and neglect spanning roughly five centuries.
Naming a single date for its end is mostly a rhetorical exercise.

The first major damage came in 48 BCE during Julius Caesar's
Alexandrian War. Caesar, besieged in the royal palace district, set
fire to the harbor's warehouse stocks; the fire spread inland and
damaged a substantial portion of the Library's holdings. Estimates
of the loss range from 40,000 to 400,000 rolls, depending on the
ancient source; modern historians lean toward the lower end.
Mark Antony's later gift to Cleopatra of 200,000 volumes from the
Pergamon library is sometimes interpreted as restitution.

The next major blow was the destruction of the Brucheion quarter
under Aurelian in 273 CE during the suppression of the Palmyrene
revolt. The exact damage to the Library's main building is
unclear, but the Mouseion as an institution did not recover; it
appears nowhere in subsequent fourth-century inscriptions.

The Serapeum, a daughter library and temple of Serapis, was
destroyed in 391 CE under the Christianizing edicts of Theodosius I.
The historian Eunapius reports that the bibliophiles of the city
gathered scrolls from the wreckage; how much of the Library proper
was housed in the Serapeum is debated.

A final, much-folkloricized chapter is the alleged destruction by
Caliph Omar's forces in 642 CE. The story — that the Caliph ordered
the books used to heat the public baths for six months — first
appears in the thirteenth century in al-Qifti's Chronicle of the
Wise. Most modern historians regard it as legend; the Library by
that date had been functionally non-existent for several centuries.

The lesson of the decline, repeatedly drawn by later
historiographers, is the danger of single-point archives. The texts
that survived did so because they had been copied and dispersed.
Centralization, however efficient, is a vulnerability.
