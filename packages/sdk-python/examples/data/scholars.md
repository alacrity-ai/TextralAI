# The scholars of Alexandria

The Library was first a research institution. Its directors —
called the Librarian, more recently the head of the Mouseion — were
themselves working scholars whose patronage attracted the leading
minds of the Hellenistic world.

The first generation included Euclid, who composed the Elements at
Alexandria around 300 BCE; Eratosthenes, who measured the
circumference of the Earth using shadow lengths at Aswan and
Alexandria; and Aristarchus of Samothrace, whose Homeric textual
criticism set the standard for philological scholarship for two
millennia.

The second generation was no less significant. Apollonius of Perga
wrote the Conics there. Hipparchus, while not strictly a member,
maintained correspondence with the Mouseion and used its
star-catalogs as the basis for his own work on precession. Galen
trained there in the second century CE, and his anatomical
writings — much of what survives of ancient medicine — drew on
the dissection traditions the Library preserved.

Critically, scholarship at Alexandria was state-funded. The
Ptolemaic dynasty paid stipends, provided lodging in the Mouseion,
and underwrote the cost of acquiring texts. Ships entering the
harbor of Alexandria were searched for books; any found were
copied for the Library and the originals returned (or, depending on
which account one believes, the originals kept and the copies
returned). The acquisition program was as much about state prestige
as scholarly ambition.

When the Library declined, the scholars dispersed. Many went to
Pergamon, whose own library had been founded as a deliberate
counterweight; others to Antioch, Athens, or Rhodes. The
intellectual diaspora is partially traceable through the
biographical entries in later compilations like the Suda, but a
great deal is lost. The model of state-funded research, however,
survived and was reproduced in every major scholarly center for the
next thousand years.
