# The Automaton of Vellsbruck

In the alpine town of Vellsbruck, in the spring of 1873, a
clockmaker named Henrik Almer finished a project he had begun
fourteen winters earlier. He called it Konder.

Konder was an automaton — a brass-and-larchwood figure the size of
a large dog, seated at a miniature harpsichord. Inside its narrow
chest were two thousand and forty-seven hand-cut gears, each one
filed by Almer himself in the workshop above the cathedral square.
The mainspring, wound by a key the size of a man's forearm, drove
Konder for exactly seventy-three minutes before falling silent.

Every day at eleven o'clock, the cathedral bell would strike
once for the late morning prayer, and at the same instant Konder
would begin to play. The melody — a single piece called *Verse for
Saskia*, written by Almer in 1859 for a daughter who had not lived
past her fourth winter — lasted four minutes and twenty seconds.
The townspeople gathered in the square below, even in the
heaviest snow, to listen.

In the autumn of Almer's seventy-second year, a representative
from the Imperial Conservatory in the city of Korenwald travelled
to Vellsbruck to offer two thousand kronen for the automaton, plus
a stipend for Almer to lecture at the conservatory. Almer
declined. The letter he wrote in reply, preserved today in the
Vellsbruck town archive under reference 1881-A-44, read in part:
"Konder is not for sale. He plays for Saskia, and Saskia stays
here."

Almer died in 1884. Konder continued to play *Verse for Saskia*
every morning at eleven for another forty-one years, until the
mainspring finally cracked in the winter of 1925. The automaton
sits today in a glass case in the south transept of the
cathedral, silent.
